Jyotisha means the knowledge of light, and it is the old Indian study of the sky. For thousands of years people here watched the sun, the moon, the planets and the stars, wrote down exactly where they were, and worked out how they move. That careful watching gave India its calendar, its festival dates, its planting seasons and its way of finding direction at night. Jyotisha has two sides that are worth keeping separate in your mind. One side works out where things in the sky actually are and when they will get there, and that is astronomy, checkable by anybody with a clear night. The other side reads meaning into those positions for a person's life, and that is astrology, which is a matter of belief and tradition rather than something a measurement can settle.

The panchanga is the traditional Indian almanac, and its name simply means five limbs. Those five parts are the tithi, the vara, the nakshatra, the yoga and the karana, and together they describe any given day completely in the old system. A village priest reading a panchanga can tell you which lunar day it is, which weekday, which star the moon is sitting in, and which combinations apply. It is still printed and still used, in homes across India, to fix the date of a wedding or a festival. What makes it remarkable is that all five quantities are worked out from the positions of just two objects, the sun and the moon, tracked with great care over centuries.

A tithi is a lunar day, and it is not the same thing as the day you get from the sun. A tithi is the time the moon takes to pull twelve degrees further ahead of the sun in the sky. Thirty tithis make one full cycle from new moon to new moon and back. Because the moon does not travel at a perfectly even speed, a tithi is not a fixed length. It runs anywhere from about twenty hours to about twenty seven. That is why a festival tied to a tithi can seem to shift about on an ordinary calendar, and why a panchanga tells you not just the tithi but the exact hour it begins and ends.

The seven days of the week are named after the sun, the moon and the five planets you can see without a telescope. Ravivar is the sun's day, Somvar the moon's, Mangalvar takes Mars, Budhvar Mercury, Guruvar Jupiter, Shukravar Venus and Shanivar Saturn. Now look at English. Sunday is the sun, Monday the moon, and Saturday is Saturn. In French, Mardi is Mars and Mercredi is Mercury. The same seven bodies, in the same order, name the week across cultures that were separated by enormous distances. It is one of the oldest ideas humans still use every single day.

A nakshatra is a patch of sky the moon passes through, and the old system divides the moon's whole path into twenty seven of them. The moon goes right round the sky in about twenty seven days, so it moves through roughly one nakshatra each day, which is exactly why there are twenty seven. Each one is named after the star pattern in it, like Rohini, Ashwini or Chitra. The nakshatra the moon was in when a child was born is that child's birth star, and in many families it decides the first syllable of the child's name. If you know your nakshatra, you know where the moon stood on the night you were born.

A rashi is a sign, and there are twelve of them dividing the sun's yearly path around the sky. The sun appears to travel right round the sky once a year, always along the same line, and that line is called the ecliptic. Cut it into twelve equal parts and you have the twelve rashis, from Mesha through to Meena. The sun spends about a month in each. Twelve rashis and twenty seven nakshatras are two different rulers laid along the same road, one convenient for the sun and one convenient for the moon, and a panchanga uses both at once.

The navagraha are the nine grahas of the tradition, and graha does not really mean planet. It means one that seizes or takes hold, so the word is about influence rather than about being a world. The nine are the sun, the moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, Saturn, and then Rahu and Ketu. Notice what is in that list and what is not. The sun is a star and the moon is our satellite, yet both are counted, because the list is of things that move against the fixed stars as seen from earth. Uranus and Neptune are missing for a very good reason. You cannot see them with your eyes alone.

Rahu and Ketu are the two that surprise people, because they are not objects at all. They are points. The moon's path around the earth is tilted a little against the sun's path, so the two paths cross at exactly two places, on opposite sides of the sky. Those two crossing points are Rahu and Ketu. They matter enormously because eclipses can only happen when the sun and moon meet near one of them. The old story says a demon swallows the sun, and the demon's head and tail are these two points. Underneath the story sits real geometry, and that geometry predicts eclipses correctly.

An eclipse is a shadow, and Indian astronomers said so a very long time ago. Aryabhata, writing about fifteen hundred years ago, stated plainly that the moon has no light of its own, that a lunar eclipse is the earth's shadow falling on the moon, and that a solar eclipse is the moon's shadow falling on the earth. He also said the earth spins on its axis, and that the stars only seem to wheel overhead because we are the ones turning. He worked all this out with no telescope, from patient measurement and hard arithmetic, and he was right.

The Indian calendar is lunisolar, which means it tries to keep step with the moon and the sun at the same time. Twelve lunar months come to about three hundred and fifty four days, while the year the seasons follow is about three hundred and sixty five. Left alone, the months would drift backwards through the seasons and a monsoon festival would eventually land in winter. The fix is an extra month, the adhika masa, slipped in roughly every third year to catch the calendar up. That is why Diwali does not fall on the same English date each year, but never wanders far from autumn either.

Sankranti means the sun crossing over from one rashi into the next, and it happens twelve times a year. Makar Sankranti, when the sun enters Makara, is the famous one, and it is celebrated as Pongal, Lohri, Bihu and Uttarayan depending on where you live. Here is the thing worth noticing. Makar Sankranti sits on about the fourteenth of January every single year, while Diwali and Holi move around by weeks. The reason is simple once you know it. Sankranti is reckoned from the sun, so it matches the English calendar, which is also solar. The moving festivals are reckoned from the moon.

Precession is the slow wobble of the earth, and it is the reason two zodiacs exist. The earth spins like a top, and like a top it also leans round very slowly, taking about twenty six thousand years for one full wobble. So the direction the earth's axis points keeps changing, and the whole sky appears to creep gradually against the seasons. Indian jyotisha measures the signs against the actual stars, which is called nirayana. Western astrology measures them against the seasons, which is called sayana. The gap between the two is now about twenty four degrees, which is why your Indian sign is usually the one before your Western one.

The ayanamsa is the number that measures that gap, and keeping it right is real astronomical work. Because the wobble is slow but never stops, the difference between the star zodiac and the season zodiac grows by about one degree every seventy two years. An almanac maker has to know the current value to place the rashis correctly, and Indian astronomers have tracked and argued over it for centuries. Around the fifth century the two zodiacs lined up almost exactly and the ayanamsa was near zero. Everything since is the slow drift of a spinning planet, written down carefully by people who noticed.

Aryabhata was not alone, and the line of Indian astronomers ran for well over a thousand years. Varahamihira gathered the knowledge of his time into one great compilation. Brahmagupta worked out rules for calculating with zero and with negative numbers, which mathematics everywhere still uses. Bhaskara the second wrote on the motion of planets and on ideas that come close to the calculus of much later centuries. In Kerala, Madhava and the school that followed him found infinite series for sines and for pi, some two hundred years before similar work appeared in Europe. All of it grew out of the need to predict the sky accurately.

Jantar Mantar is astronomy you can walk around inside. In the seventeen hundreds Sawai Jai Singh the second built five of these observatories, at Jaipur, Delhi, Ujjain, Mathura and Varanasi, out of stone and plaster rather than brass, because he wanted instruments big enough to be read precisely and steady enough not to warp. The Samrat Yantra at Jaipur is a sundial almost as tall as a nine storey building, and its shadow edge moves about a millimetre every second. Built with no glass and no electricity, it tells the local time to within about two seconds, and it still works today.

The old astronomers had to be right, because farming depended on it. A calendar that slipped would tell a village to sow at the wrong time, and a wrong sowing meant a lost crop and a hungry year. Knowing when the monsoon was due, when the days would start lengthening, and when the cold would break was not an academic matter, it was survival. Festivals grew up around exactly these turning points, which is why so many of them mark a harvest or a solstice. Sky watching in India began as a practical craft, and the mathematics came from the demand for accuracy.

A kundali is a birth chart, and what it actually is, is a map. It records where the sun, the moon and the visible planets stood, as seen from one place on earth, at one particular moment. That much is plain astronomy, and it can be checked and recomputed by anybody with the right method. The tradition then reads meaning into that arrangement, holding that the pattern at birth speaks to a person's nature and their life. Those two things sit on very different footings. The positions are measurements. The meanings are what the tradition teaches, and they are not the kind of claim a measurement can confirm.

It is worth being straight about what science says here. Careful modern studies have tested whether the positions of planets at birth predict personality or life events, and they have not found an effect. So the fair way to hold jyotisha is this. Its astronomy is genuinely excellent, and much of it was centuries ahead of the rest of the world. Its calendar still runs Indian life and runs it well. Its predictive side is a tradition of meaning, deeply woven into culture and family life, and you can learn it and respect it while knowing it is belief rather than tested fact. Both things are true at once, and neither one cancels the other.

Muhurta is the practice of choosing a good moment, and it is one of the most visible parts of jyotisha in daily life. Before a wedding, a housewarming, the start of a business or a child's first lesson, many families consult a panchanga to pick an auspicious time. The tradition holds that certain combinations of tithi, nakshatra and weekday favour certain undertakings. There is something quietly sensible in the custom too, whatever you make of the reasoning. It makes families stop, agree on a date together, and treat a beginning as something that deserves marking.

The zodiac is a shared human invention, and tracing it is a lovely piece of history. The idea of cutting the sun's path into twelve parts appears first in Babylon, well over two thousand years ago. It travelled to Greece, and it travelled to India, and each place shaped it in its own way while keeping the twelve. That is why an Indian rashi and a Western sign line up name for name, Mesha with Aries, Vrishabha with Taurus, all the way round. Different civilisations looked up at the same sky, divided it in the same way, and passed the idea along until it belonged to everyone.

People often ask whether the stars can tell your future, and that deserves a straight answer. The tradition holds that the sky at the moment of your birth speaks to your nature and your path, and the horoscopes printed in newspapers grew out of that idea. When scientists have tested it carefully, comparing many thousands of birth charts against real personalities and real lives, they have found no effect at all. Nothing in the sky decides whether you will pass an exam, who you will marry, or whether today is a lucky day for you. Your own choices and your own effort do that. You can enjoy the tradition, learn the beautiful mathematics behind it and keep its festivals, and still know that your future is not written in the stars.
